Parallel lines are an assumption of this model, not a finding. The additive model forces the lines to be parallel whether or not they are parallel in the population. Testing that assumption requires the product term ×, which is what step 2 adds.

Moderated regression analysis

The step 1 model is additive. Each predictor contributes its own weighted amount to the predicted value of , and the contributions are simply added together, so the – slope is the same whatever the value of . To test for an interaction, we introduce a product term, ×, formed by multiplying the two predictors. The result is a multiplicative model: Ŷ = b0 + b1 + b2 + b3(×). Because the predictors are multiplied, the contribution of depends on the value of , and vice versa. The model is still linear in its weights, so it is estimated by ordinary least squares like any other regression; “multiplicative” refers to the product of the predictors, not to the way the weights are estimated.

The additive and multiplicative components are often entered hierarchically in order to isolate the contribution of the product beyond that of its components. Here, and are entered in a first step, and the product term is entered in a second step. The interaction is tested by the increase in R² from the first step to the second (equivalently, by the t-test of the product's weight in the second-step model). If it is significant, probe it by computing the – slope at chosen values of .

A note on centering

It is recommended that predictors be centered prior to forming products. Read on to learn how and why to do this.

What it is. To mean-center a variable, subtract its mean from every person’s score on that variable. For example, centering means subtracting the mean of from each person’s score; the result is the centered variable c. A person who is exactly average now has a score of 0, a person 5 points above average has a score of +5, and so on. The centered variable has a mean of zero instead of its original mean, so its scores are now arranged around zero.

What it does not change. Centering slides every score by the same amount, so the distances among scores are unchanged. The variance, the standard deviation, the shape of the distribution, and the correlation with every other variable are all exactly what they were before centering.

Why center and before forming the product. There are three benefits.

First, the lower-order weights become meaningful. In a model with a product term, b1 is the – slope at the point where = 0. If is uncentered, 0 may be far outside the data (on a 1-to-5 rating scale, for example, no one can score 0), and b1 then describes a slope for a type of case that does not exist. Once is centered, 0 is its mean, so b1 is the – slope for a case of average . The same logic applies to b2. The intercept becomes the predicted value of for a case at the mean of both predictors.

Second, centering removes most of the correlation between the product and its components. When and are both well above zero, the product rises whenever either one rises, so the uncentered product is highly correlated with each of them. This produces large VIFs. Centering eliminates this nonessential ill-conditioning, which arises only from where zero happens to fall on each scale. (Any collinearity that remains when the predictors are skewed is essential and cannot be removed by centering.) Be clear about what this does and does not accomplish. It does not make the interaction easier to detect: b3, its standard error, and its test are identical with or without centering. The uncentered b1 and b2 do have larger standard errors, but that is because they estimate slopes at zero on the other predictor, often far from the data, not because information has been lost. The benefit is that the VIFs no longer signal a multicollinearity problem that does not exist (and, in older software, centering also reduced rounding error).

Third, b1 and b2 become average effects. Each case in the sample has its own – slope, b1 + b3·c, which depends on that case’s value of . Because centered has a mean of zero, the average of these slopes across the sample is exactly b1. So with centered predictors, b1 is both the – slope for a case of average and the average – slope across all cases, much like a main effect in analysis of variance. The same is true of b2. Without centering, b1 and b2 have neither interpretation unless the other predictor’s mean happens to be zero.

Is there a downside? Not really. Centering does not change b3, its standard error, its test, R², ΔR², the predicted values, or the simple slopes, so the evidence for the interaction is identical either way. The only cost is remembering that b1 and b2 are now slopes at the mean of the other predictor rather than slopes that apply everywhere. If a predictor already has a meaningful zero (a dummy-coded group, for example, where 0 is the reference group), leaving it uncentered is fine. More generally, a predictor can be centered at any value of interest, because the goal is simply to put zero where an interpretable case lies.

    Centering

    Centering and before forming their product changes b1, b2, their standard errors, and the VIFs. It does not change b3, its test, R², ΔR², the predicted values, or the simple slopes.

    When the predictors have means far from zero, the uncentered product is highly correlated with each of them. This is often called nonessential multicollinearity (Cohen, Cohen, West, & Aiken, 2003): it arises only because of where zero happens to fall on each scale, and it does not affect the test of the interaction. The real reason to center is interpretive. Without centering, b1 is the – slope when = 0, which may be a value no one in the sample has.

    Power to detect the interaction

    Interactions are hard to detect in field data. Their effect sizes are usually small, predictors are rarely spread out to the extremes where interactions show most clearly (McClelland & Judd, 1993), and measurement error in and is compounded in their product (Busemeyer & Jones, 1983).
